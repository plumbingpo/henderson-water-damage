Water Heater Leak Cleanup in Henderson: What to Know

The tank on a water heater stores 40 to 80 gallons, yet a leak can release far more than that. Because the supply line feeds the tank, a rusted out bottom drains and refills at the same pace. Until someone closes the cold water valve, it keeps running. The location of the heater sets the damage. It may be a hallway closet, a garage floor or, the worst case, an attic over finished rooms.

After extracting the water, our Henderson crew checks the materials that sat next to the unit. Closet walls and the platform under the tank are usually soaked at the bottom. In a garage, water reaches the wall shared with the house and anything stored on the floor. From an attic it comes down through insulation and ceilings. Each of those areas is metered, then dried using dehumidifiers and air movers.

First Five Minutes

Before We Arrive

  1. Turn Off the Heat SourceElectric units get shut down at the breaker. With a gas heater, the knob on the control goes to the off position. Running the burner or the element with no water in the tank is something to avoid every time.
  2. Close the Cold SupplyFind the cold water shutoff above the heater and turn it clockwise. If it is stuck or leaking, shut off the main water valve for the house.
  3. Watch for Hot WaterWater from the tank can scald. Pets and children should stay clear, and nobody should walk into pooled water before it cools down.
  4. Drain the Tank SafelyIf you can reach the drain valve, attach a garden hose and run it outside or to a floor drain so the remaining water does not empty onto the floor.
  5. Move Stored ItemsLift boxes, rugs and anything absorbent off the wet floor. Because cardboard and fabric take in water quickly, they hold dampness against the walls and the slab.

Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • Drain valves that crack, work loose or stop sealing completely.
  • Drain pans beneath closet or attic units that rust through or clog and then spill over.
  • Loose plumbing connections left after a recent installation or repair.
  • Corroded inlet and outlet fittings or flex connectors on top of the heater.
  • Excess pressure in the tank from a failed thermostat or a missing expansion tank.
If It Is Left

Where the Damage Spreads

Heater Closet Drywall

Drywall surrounding a heater closet inside the house wicks water up and lets it through to the neighboring rooms. Swollen baseboards and a wall bottom that is still damp within are typical.

Garage Wall Base

The wall between the garage and the house takes water into its bottom plate and drywall from the slab, and from there it reaches the floors of the living space.

Ceilings Under an Attic Heater

Water from an attic unit runs down through insulation to the ceiling beneath it. Wet insulation stops insulating and its weight can bring ceiling drywall down.

Stand and Subfloor Damage

Under the tank, the wood stand or the subfloor absorbs the largest share of the leak. Rot can weaken it, a real concern once a full new tank sits on top.

Will Insurance Pay?

Does Insurance Cover It?

When a tank fails suddenly, the water damage to floors, walls and belongings is often covered as sudden and accidental. Replacing the heater is usually not included, because a tank wearing out from age counts as maintenance. If an adjuster finds signs of a long-term leak, such as rust trails or old staining, the claim can be reduced or denied because gradual leaks usually are not covered. Check your policy, photograph the heater and the damage, and keep the old unit until the claim is settled.

What It Costs

What Drives the Price

  • Any rebuilding of the subfloor or platform beneath the tank ahead of the new heater.
  • If water got to the flooring of adjoining rooms, and the kind of flooring involved.
  • How long the tank kept refilling before the supply valve was closed.
  • Where the heater sits, with attic and interior closet leaks costing more than garage leaks.
